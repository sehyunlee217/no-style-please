I haven’t written much for myself since high school. Writing was something I found difficult, and I gradually stopped making time for it.

Recently, I was preparing for an art history assessment that would take place on paper. I sat down to practice writing a few paragraphs without AI. It took me over an hour to produce a draft I was reasonably happy with.

What surprised me was how much I enjoyed it. Finding the words was slow, but the process helped me figure out what I actually thought.

It made me wonder, when is efficiency the right goal?

### Using AI to learn

AI has become a useful part of how I work. It helps me explore coding approaches, work through difficult concepts, and catch mistakes. When I’m stuck, having something to question and work through examples with can help me move forward, though I still need to check its answers.

But moving forward quickly doesn’t always mean I’ve learned what I wanted to learn.

I noticed this while working on a personal project about Toronto’s bike-share system. I bike to school and around downtown, and I often find the station near home empty or the station at my destination full. I wanted to understand these patterns and see whether I could plan my trips better.

With AI, I quickly put together a machine-learning prototype. It appeared to work, but I couldn’t yet explain enough about the data, the model’s assumptions, or how to judge its predictions.

That gap mattered because understanding the system was what had drawn me to the project. Having a working prototype answered only part of my question.

The experience helped me see a distinction I hadn’t paid enough attention to. Sometimes we want a result, and sometimes we want the understanding that comes from working toward it. Knowing which one we're after changes how we should use the tools available.

### What makes something valuable?

A [post about the value of things](https://journal.stuffwithstuff.com/2026/01/24/the-value-of-things/) helped me put words to a related feeling.

In high school, I spent a lot of time making art. I mostly drew and painted, but I also tried pottery. My mom likes collecting bowls, so before going home for summer break, I would make a few to bring back.

They weren’t particularly great. They were heavier than the bowls sold in ceramic shops, and the glaze was sometimes uneven.

Still, those bowls mean something to us. I remember making them and choosing which ones to bring home. Their imperfections are part of that memory.

Spending more time on something doesn’t automatically make it better. But the time we choose to give it can become part of its value.

### Leaving room for the process

As an industrial engineering student, I spend a lot of time thinking about how to make things more efficient. I enjoy that work. Removing unnecessary effort can give people more time for things they care about.

I’m also learning to ask what I want to preserve in the process.

For a personal project, that might be the time spent exploring an unexpected pattern in the data. For writing, it might be struggling with a sentence until I understand the thought behind it. For pottery, it was making something with someone in mind.

I still want to use AI and other tools to work well. I also want to be deliberate about where I spend the time they save.

Sometimes, taking longer is a choice I’m happy to make.
